What counts as wildlife hazard management?

ICAO guidance frames wildlife hazard management as a structured programme: identify hazards, collect and analyse wildlife and strike data, manage habitat and land use, use dispersal/repellent methods, train personnel and continuously evaluate whether controls are effective. EASA Regulation (EU) No 139/2014 similarly requires ongoing evaluation of wildlife hazards at aerodromes and monitoring of surrounding land uses that can encourage hazardous wildlife activity.

Birds are not all the same risk

Risk depends on more than presence. Species, body mass, flock size, flight behaviour, altitude, location relative to runway/approach paths, time of day and phase of aircraft operation all matter. A small passerine away from the movement area may present less risk than a flock of large gulls or geese crossing the climb-out path.

Learning checkpoint 1

Risk starts with ecology and behaviour

1. Hazard is not the same as presence

A wildlife inventory tells you what exists; a hazard assessment asks which animals can realistically interact with aircraft. Large flocking birds, waterfowl, gulls and raptors may create disproportionate strike severity because body mass and group behaviour affect impact energy and the probability of multiple ingestion/impact events. Location is equally important: the same species can be low risk in a remote corner and high risk when routinely crossing runway or climb-out paths.

2. Build the source–attractant–movement model

Ask why wildlife is present: food, water, shelter, roosting, nesting, safety from predators or simply a convenient movement corridor. Then ask how that attraction connects to aircraft paths. The purpose of the model is to identify a manageable cause rather than repeatedly treating the visible symptom.

3. Use strike and observation data together

Strike reports can underestimate risk if reporting is incomplete, while sightings alone can exaggerate risk if all species are treated equally. Strong programmes combine strike history, species identification, carcass/remains data, patrol observations, seasonal patterns, time-of-day patterns and operational context.

4. Surrounding land use matters

EASA requires monitoring of developments that may encourage harmful wildlife activity around aerodromes. Waste facilities, standing water, wetlands, sewage treatment, certain landscaping and other land uses can generate regular bird movements across the airfield even when the attractant itself is outside the perimeter.

Learning checkpoint 2

Habitat management reduces the need to chase birds

1. Food, water, shelter and security

Wildlife stays where the environment rewards it. Standing water can attract waterfowl, gulls and wading birds. Short turf can expose invertebrates or provide grazing for some species; taller or poorly managed vegetation can create cover for small mammals that attract raptors. Waste, seeds, berries, roof structures, drainage basins and disturbed soils can all modify the wildlife community.

2. Grass height must be evidence-based

CAP 772 describes long-grass management as an established UK method and cites 200–300 mm as historically effective for reducing many hazardous species, while also stating clearly that environmental, climatic and operational differences make a single universal rule inappropriate. Other jurisdictions and species assemblages can require different regimes. The correct learning is not “always cut to X cm”; it is “manage vegetation for the hazardous species actually present, then verify the result.”

3. Water management is one of the strongest levers

FAA guidance identifies standing water as a strong attractant and recommends eliminating it as far as practical, improving drainage and designing detention systems to avoid persistent wet conditions where feasible. Where water cannot be eliminated, physical exclusion or other design solutions may reduce its attractiveness, subject to engineering and emergency-access constraints.

4. Biodiversity features need safeguarding design

Wildlife hazard management does not mean banning biodiversity. UK CAA safeguarding guidance explicitly notes that biodiversity enhancements and bird-strike risk reduction can coexist if designed correctly. The question is whether an enhancement increases the abundance or movement of hazardous species in critical aviation zones. A pollinator feature far from flight paths may have a different risk profile from a large open water body that attracts flocking waterfowl.

5. Habitat work should change measurable behaviour

A habitat action is successful when the hazardous species uses the critical area less often, spends less time there, or no longer crosses aircraft paths as frequently. Measure the ecological response: sightings per patrol, flock size, time-in-zone, recurring species, strike rate/severity, attractant persistence and seasonal trends. “We mowed the grass” is an activity; “large gull presence in the departure-side verge fell 60% during the target season without shifting the problem to another critical zone” is a performance statement.

Learning checkpoint 3

Deterrence works best as an adaptive toolbox

1. Habituation is the enemy of routine

Birds learn. A stimulus that initially causes escape may lose effect if it is repeated predictably and never followed by a meaningful consequence. Good programmes vary methods, match them to species behaviour and combine dispersal with habitat measures so animals do not immediately return to the same food or shelter reward.

2. Falconry is a specialist biological signal

Trained raptors can create a credible predation threat and can be effective for some species and sites. But performance depends on target species, weather, visibility, operator skill, airfield layout and how the method is integrated with other controls. It should not be presented as a universal replacement for habitat management or risk assessment.

3. UAS/drones require aviation integration

FAA guidance on UAS for wildlife dispersal emphasises coordination with the airport operator, airspace authorisation and relevant wildlife permits before operations. The reason is obvious: an uncoordinated drone near a runway can itself become an airborne conflict. In this game, UAS is therefore an advanced conditional tool, never the default first response.

4. Repellents and aversive tools are species-specific

Vehicle approach, distress calls, pyrotechnics, acoustic devices, dogs, lasers and other deterrents have different effectiveness, legal constraints and operational limitations. Some can disturb non-target species or neighbouring communities. The wildlife-management plan should define authorised tools, competence requirements, safe-use zones and stop criteria.

5. The dispersal action is incomplete until the risk is re-assessed

“Birds moved” is not enough. Where did they move? Did they cross the runway? Did the flock split into smaller groups? Did they settle on the opposite side of the movement area? Did they return after two minutes? A professional wildlife operator observes the post-dispersal behaviour and only then supports an operational release decision.

Learning checkpoint 4

Protected species do not remove the duty to manage safety

1. The legal baseline in the EU

The EU Birds Directive establishes a general protection system for wild birds, including prohibitions on deliberate killing/capture, deliberate destruction or damage to nests and eggs, and significant deliberate disturbance especially during breeding and rearing. Derogations can exist under specified conditions, but they are not something an operator should assume on the spot.

2. Separate the immediate risk from the long-term habitat fix

If an active nest or protected species creates a current flight-safety concern, the operational response may include temporary restrictions, patrol intensification, rerouting or other locally authorised controls while legal/ecological specialists determine what wildlife action is permitted. After the sensitive period, building design, exclusion and habitat management can reduce the chance of re-establishment.

3. Preventing establishment is often easier than removing it

Bird-proofing ledges, closing access points and managing attractive roof features before the breeding season can be more effective and legally simpler than dealing with an active nest. Prevention should therefore be built into facilities management and maintenance planning.

4. Military context still needs legal clarity

This simulator uses civil international/European guidance as a technical reference. A military airbase may operate under different national military aviation regulations and specific defence derogations. The correct professional behaviour is to verify the applicable regime, not to assume that civil rules automatically apply—or that military status automatically removes wildlife-protection obligations.

5. The false choice to avoid

“Either protect birds or protect aircraft” is poor management framing. The better question is: how do we keep high-risk wildlife out of critical aviation zones while maintaining lawful conservation outcomes elsewhere? That can mean relocating biodiversity enhancement away from high-risk corridors, designing buildings to resist nesting, managing water differently and coordinating land use beyond the perimeter.

Learning checkpoint 5

Wildlife risk is dynamic

1. A runway can be clear and still be unsafe

Birds may be outside the runway surface but moving toward it, crossing the climb-out path, or likely to take flight when disturbed by an approaching aircraft. The assessment therefore considers position, trajectory, flock size, species, wind, time and aircraft phase—not just whether the pavement is visually empty.

2. Operational thresholds should be pre-defined

The best time to decide what constitutes unacceptable wildlife risk is before the event. Wildlife management plans should define responsibilities, communication channels, escalation routes and locally appropriate thresholds or decision criteria so that operators do not improvise under schedule pressure.

3. Holding can be the most efficient option

A short delay that allows a flock to be dispersed and the area re-checked can protect safety while preserving mission continuity. Conversely, repeated departures through known high-risk wildlife activity can create avoidable damage, inspection burden and operational disruption.

4. Reporting closes the loop

Wildlife strikes and significant wildlife events should be recorded through the applicable system. EASA requires national procedures for wildlife-strike reporting and ongoing evaluation. ICAO maintains the IBIS framework. Data quality matters because future habitat and patrol decisions depend on what is actually reported.

Advanced knowledge base

Wildlife hazard management for air-base operations

This section goes beyond the game choices so the app can be used as a genuine learning resource.

1. Why wildlife strikes are a system risk

Wildlife strike risk emerges from the interaction between ecology, infrastructure, flight operations and human decision-making. A base can have excellent dispersal staff but still perform poorly if drainage attracts waterfowl, waste feeds gulls, roofs provide nesting habitat or surrounding land use creates daily flight corridors across the runway. The management system therefore has to combine environmental and aviation-safety thinking.

2. ICAO Wildlife Hazard Management Programme

ICAO's Airport Services Manual Part III and PANS-Aerodromes guidance emphasise a programme specific to each aerodrome. Core elements include hazard assessment, habitat/land-use management, reporting and recording, wildlife expulsion/deterrence, personnel training and continuous evaluation. The plan should be based on the species and ecological conditions actually present.

3. EASA regulatory context

Regulation (EU) No 139/2014 requires Member States to ensure that wildlife strike hazards are assessed using strike reporting, information on hazardous wildlife presence and ongoing evaluation by competent personnel. It also requires monitoring of land uses around aerodromes that may encourage wildlife activity harmful to aircraft operations. These provisions apply to the civil aerodrome framework; military applicability must be checked separately.

4. Habitat management: the preventive layer

Habitat management aims to reduce the attractiveness of the airfield before hazardous wildlife establishes regular use. Typical levers include vegetation structure, drainage, waste management, food availability, building design, waterbody configuration and control of small-mammal prey. The correct design is species-specific and should be evaluated through monitoring.

5. Vegetation and grass height

Vegetation can affect visibility, food availability, nesting cover, grazing opportunities and prey abundance. UK CAP 772 describes long-grass management as often effective and cites a historical 200–300 mm range, while explicitly rejecting a one-size-fits-all approach. A different region, target species or safety constraint may require different management. Always use local wildlife evidence and approved aerodrome procedures.

6. Water is a powerful attractant

Standing water provides drinking, bathing, feeding and refuge opportunities. FAA guidance recommends removing persistent standing water as far as practical and designing drainage/detention features so they do not create attractive long-lived waterbodies. Where water is unavoidable, design, physical exclusion and active management may reduce risk.

7. Buildings and nesting

Flat roofs, ledges, cavities, hangars and other structures can provide safe nesting or roosting sites. Preventive exclusion before nesting is established is often easier than intervention after an active nest appears. Facilities teams therefore have a role in wildlife hazard management: bird-proofing, access closure and maintenance timing can be part of the control strategy.

8. Dispersal and habituation

Active deterrence works when wildlife perceives the stimulus as a credible reason to leave. Repetition without consequence produces habituation. Programmes therefore combine methods, vary timing, understand species behaviour and remove the ecological reward that draws wildlife back. The goal is sustained reduction in hazardous zone use, not a momentary flush.

9. Falconry

Falconry can simulate predation pressure and may be effective for some flocking species. It requires specialist operators, welfare standards, operational coordination and a clear understanding of target species. Weather, raptor condition, airfield geometry and competing wildlife behaviour influence results. It is best treated as one component of an integrated programme.

10. UAS / drone dispersal

FAA CertAlert 23-08 and related guidance show why UAS wildlife dispersal needs formal integration: the airport operator and UAS operator must coordinate, necessary airspace approvals are required, and wildlife permits may also apply. A drone that is not segregated from aircraft operations adds an aviation hazard while trying to remove another one.

11. Avian radar

Avian radar can help detect and characterise bird movements, especially recurring flight lines, altitude bands and temporal patterns. It does not replace trained observation or species identification. Radar data are most useful when combined with field validation, strike records and decision thresholds so that the system changes operational behaviour rather than simply generating more data.

12. Protected species and nesting law

In the EU, the Birds Directive establishes protection against deliberate killing/capture, destruction or damage of nests/eggs, and significant deliberate disturbance during breeding and rearing. Derogations can exist for specified reasons under controlled conditions. An aviation-safety need therefore has to be handled through the applicable lawful process, not by informal nest removal.

13. Biodiversity and aerodrome safeguarding can coexist

CAA safeguarding guidance explicitly recognises that biodiversity enhancement and wildlife-strike mitigation can coexist if designed correctly. The critical question is whether an intervention increases net risk to aircraft. Enhancement should therefore be located and designed to favour ecological value without attracting large flocking or heavy species into approach, departure or runway corridors.

14. Reporting and learning

Strike data enable trend analysis, hotspot identification and evaluation of control effectiveness. Reports should capture species where possible, location, aircraft phase, damage, weather/time and other relevant factors. Near-miss or high-activity observations can also be valuable where the local management system uses them consistently.

15. Metrics that actually help

Useful metrics include hazardous-bird observations per patrol hour, flock size in critical zones, time spent in runway strip, recurrence after dispersal, strike rate by movement, damaging-strike severity, persistent attractants closed, response time and proportion of identified high-risk land-use issues addressed. Metrics should support decisions, not merely produce dashboards.

16. Military / Air Power use

Military operations can add low-level routes, unusual schedules, deployed environments and mission-readiness pressures. The same ecological principles remain useful, but legal authority, military aviation regulation, host-nation rules, operational security and command responsibilities may differ from civil aerodrome practice. The plan must be adapted to the actual authority and operating context.
